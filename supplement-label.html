What Should You Look for on a Supplement Label?

Supplement labels contain important information about serving size, ingredients, amounts, directions and other product details. Learn how to read a supplement label more carefully and understand what the information can and cannot tell you about a product.

Walking into a pharmacy, supermarket or health store can mean facing shelves filled with vitamins, minerals, herbal products, protein powders and other dietary supplements. Many products make attractive claims on the front of the package, but some of the most useful information is found on the back. The supplement label can tell you what ingredients are provided, how much is in a serving, how many servings the container contains and how the product is intended to be used.

Learning to read this information is important because a supplement is not simply identified by the large words printed on the front of its bottle. The front may highlight a particular nutrient, herb or marketing claim, while the Supplement Facts panel provides more detailed information about the contents.

Why Should You Read a Supplement Label?

A dietary supplement label is designed to provide information that consumers can use when deciding whether a product is appropriate for them. In the United States, required labeling includes information such as the product identity, net quantity of contents, manufacturer or distributor information, directions for use and a Supplement Facts panel. The label also includes an "Other Ingredients" section when applicable. 1

Reading the label can help you avoid a common mistake: assuming that the amount of a nutrient named on the front of a bottle is the amount you will actually consume in one serving. The serving size and amount per serving are particularly important.

1. Start With the Supplement Facts Panel

One of the first places to look is the panel labeled Supplement Facts. This section provides information about the dietary ingredients in the product and the amount provided per serving. Depending on the ingredient, the label may also provide a percentage of the Daily Value, when a Daily Value has been established. 2

Do not judge a supplement only by the number of ingredients listed. A long ingredient list does not automatically mean a product is better, and a short list does not automatically mean it is safer or more effective.

Instead, look at the identity and quantity of each dietary ingredient and compare that information with what you actually need.

2. Check the Serving Size

Serving size is one of the easiest parts of a label to overlook. It is also one of the most important.

For example, imagine a bottle contains 60 capsules. The Supplement Facts panel might state that one serving is two capsules. In that situation, the amounts listed in the panel generally describe what is provided by two capsules, not one.

This matters when comparing two products. A supplement that appears to contain more of a nutrient may simply have a larger serving size.

FDA guidance explains that the serving size on a dietary supplement label represents the maximum amount recommended, as appropriate, for consumption at one eating occasion, or, when there is no recommendation, one unit such as a tablet, capsule or teaspoonful. 3

Always check whether the serving is one capsule, two capsules, one scoop, two scoops, one teaspoon or another quantity before comparing products.

3. Look at the Amount Per Serving

After checking serving size, examine the amount of each dietary ingredient provided per serving.

Vitamins and minerals may be listed in units such as milligrams (mg) or micrograms (mcg). Other ingredients may be listed using different units depending on their nature.

The number printed next to an ingredient is therefore more useful when you understand the unit and know whether the amount applies to one serving or to the entire container.

For example, a label stating that a product contains 500 mg of a particular nutrient per serving is very different from a product containing 500 mg in the entire bottle.

A supplement label should be read from the serving size downward—not just from the biggest claim on the front of the package.

4. Understand the Percent Daily Value (%DV)

The % Daily Value, commonly written as %DV, tells you how much a nutrient in one serving contributes to the Daily Value established for that nutrient.

The Daily Value is a reference amount used on food and supplement labels. It is not necessarily identical to an individual's personal nutritional requirement. Nutrient requirements can vary according to factors such as age, sex and life stage.

For example, the FDA Daily Value for vitamin C is 90 mg, while the Daily Value for iron is 18 mg for adults and children age four and older under the standard labeling framework. These are labeling reference values and should not be interpreted as personalized medical prescriptions.

As a general labeling guide, 5% DV or less is considered low and 20% DV or more is considered high for a nutrient in a serving. However, these percentages should not be interpreted to mean that a higher amount is always better.

Some nutrients can cause problems when consumed in excessive amounts, particularly when supplements are combined or taken inappropriately.

5. Do Not Assume "More" Means Better

Supplement marketing often emphasizes large numbers, high potency or multiple ingredients. But a higher amount is not automatically more beneficial.

The appropriate amount of a nutrient depends on the nutrient itself, the individual's nutritional status, diet, age, health circumstances and other factors.

Taking several products can also result in overlapping ingredients. For example, a person might take a multivitamin, an individual vitamin D supplement and another fortified product without realizing that the same nutrient appears in more than one source.

This is why the amount per serving should be considered together with everything else you regularly consume, including other supplements and fortified foods.

6. Check the Full Ingredient List

The Supplement Facts panel is not necessarily the only part of the label that contains ingredients.

Dietary supplements can also have an Other Ingredients section. This can include substances such as fillers, binders, flavors, sweeteners, colors, stabilizers and other ingredients used in making the product.

Ingredients that are not declared in the Supplement Facts panel may be listed in the Other Ingredients section. Examples can include gelatin, starches, flavors, preservatives, stabilizers and color additives.

If you have a known allergy, intolerance or other dietary restriction, checking this section can be especially important.

7. Look Carefully at Proprietary Blends

Proprietary blends can make supplement labels more difficult to evaluate.

A proprietary blend is a combination of ingredients that is identified by a specific name and for which the label may provide a total amount for the blend rather than revealing the exact amount of every individual ingredient.

For example, a label might list several herbs, botanical ingredients or other substances under one blend name and then provide one total weight for the combination.

This means a consumer may not be able to determine exactly how much of each individual ingredient is present from the label alone.

If a product contains a proprietary blend, look at the complete list of ingredients and the total amount provided. Be cautious about assuming that every ingredient in the blend is present in a clinically meaningful amount.

8. Identify Vitamins and Minerals by Their Actual Amounts

When looking at a vitamin or mineral supplement, pay attention to the actual amount of the nutrient rather than relying only on words such as "high potency," "maximum strength" or "mega."

For example, two multivitamins may both contain vitamin B12, but the amount supplied by each serving can be very different.

The same applies to nutrients such as vitamin D, calcium, magnesium, zinc, iron and folate.

The chemical form can also matter. Some nutrients appear in different forms, and the label may identify the specific form used.

Reading the actual ingredient name and amount gives you more useful information than relying on the product's marketing description.

9. Pay Attention to Botanical Names and Extracts

Herbal and botanical supplements require additional attention because a common plant name may not provide enough information to identify exactly what is being used.

A label may provide the common name of an herb as well as its botanical name. It may also identify a particular plant part, such as the root, leaf, flower, bark or seed.

Extracts can also be described using information about concentration or standardization. However, consumers should not assume that a concentrated extract is automatically safer or more effective than the original plant material.

Herbal products can interact with medicines and may not be appropriate for everyone. People who take prescription medicines should discuss herbal or botanical supplements with an appropriate healthcare professional.

10. Check the Directions for Use

The label should provide directions explaining how the manufacturer recommends using the product.

Directions may specify how many capsules, tablets, gummies or scoops should be taken and may also explain whether the product should be taken with food or water.

Do not automatically assume that taking more than the recommended amount will produce greater benefits.

Increasing the amount can also increase your exposure to the active ingredients and may increase the risk of unwanted effects.

If a healthcare professional has recommended a specific amount for you, follow their advice rather than changing the dose yourself.

11. Read the Warning and Safety Information

Do not skip the warning section of a supplement label.

Some products include warnings about pregnancy, breastfeeding, children, allergies, medical conditions, interactions with medicines or other circumstances in which the product should not be used.

Dietary supplements can interact with medications. Some supplements may also affect laboratory tests, surgery or existing health conditions.

This is particularly important if you take prescription medicines, have a chronic medical condition, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or are preparing for a medical procedure.

A supplement can contain useful nutrients and still be inappropriate for a particular person.

12. Look for the Manufacturer or Distributor Information

A supplement label should provide information that identifies the responsible company, such as the name and place of business of the manufacturer, packer or distributor.

This information can be useful when you need to ask questions about ingredients, directions, quality, product complaints or other concerns.

Be cautious when buying products where the identity of the responsible company is difficult to determine or where important labeling information appears to be missing.

13. Check the Net Quantity of Contents

The net quantity tells you how much product is contained in the package.

For tablets and capsules, this may appear as a count. For powders or liquids, it may be expressed using weight or volume.

Do not confuse the number of tablets in a bottle with the number of servings.

For example, a bottle containing 60 capsules provides 30 servings if the stated serving size is two capsules.

Looking at both the net quantity and serving size makes it easier to understand how long a container is intended to last.

14. Check the Expiration or Best-By Information

Some supplement products display expiration or best-by information. If such a date is provided, consumers should pay attention to it.

Storage conditions can also affect product quality. Some supplements may need to be protected from heat, moisture or direct sunlight.

Follow the storage instructions provided by the manufacturer and keep supplements away from children.

A supplement should not be treated like an ordinary food product simply because it is sold in a bottle or package.

15. Be Careful With "Natural" Claims

The word "natural" can sound reassuring, but it does not by itself prove that a supplement is safe, effective or appropriate for everyone.

Many substances that occur naturally can have powerful effects on the body. Some natural substances can also interact with prescription medicines or cause unwanted effects.

For this reason, do not use the word "natural" as a substitute for checking the actual ingredients, amounts, warnings and directions on the label.

The same principle applies to terms such as "herbal," "plant-based," "traditional" or "organic." These descriptions do not automatically establish that a supplement will provide a particular health benefit.

16. Understand What Health Claims Actually Mean

Supplement packages may contain statements about health, wellness, energy, immunity, digestion, performance or other benefits.

It is important to distinguish between a marketing statement and evidence that a product actually produces a particular health outcome.

In the United States, dietary supplements can make certain structure/function claims when they meet applicable requirements. These claims describe the role of a nutrient or dietary ingredient in affecting the structure or function of the body.

For example, a product might describe a nutrient as supporting normal immune function. That type of statement is different from claiming that a product diagnoses, treats, cures or prevents a specific disease.

Consumers should therefore look beyond the front of the package and consider the evidence for the specific ingredient and the amount provided.

17. Be Skeptical of Disease-Cure Claims

Be especially cautious when a supplement is advertised as a guaranteed treatment or cure for a serious disease.

Claims that a supplement can cure cancer, reverse diabetes, eliminate infections, replace prescription medicines or treat multiple unrelated diseases should be approached with particular caution.

A supplement should not be used to replace prescribed treatment without discussing the decision with a qualified healthcare professional.

The fact that an ingredient has been studied does not automatically mean that every supplement containing that ingredient has been proven to treat a disease.

18. Look at the Form of the Nutrient

Some vitamins and minerals can appear on labels in different chemical forms.

For example, magnesium supplements may contain forms such as magnesium citrate, magnesium oxide or other magnesium compounds. Different forms can have different characteristics, including differences in elemental nutrient content and how they are absorbed or tolerated.

Similarly, vitamin B12 may be listed in forms such as cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin.

This does not mean that one form is automatically the best choice for everyone. The appropriate form can depend on the particular nutrient, reason for use, diet, medications and individual circumstances.

If you are unsure which form is appropriate, ask a qualified healthcare professional or pharmacist rather than choosing based solely on marketing language.

19. Check Whether the Label Lists the Specific Amount

A useful label should allow you to determine how much of an active dietary ingredient is supplied by a serving.

This becomes particularly important with products containing multiple vitamins, minerals, herbs or other dietary ingredients.

If a product emphasizes several ingredients but does not make the amount of each ingredient clear, it can be more difficult to determine what you are actually consuming.

When comparing products, compare the amounts per serving rather than simply comparing the number of ingredients or the size of the bottle.

20. Pay Attention to Combination Products

Some supplements contain many nutrients or active ingredients in a single product.

Combination products can be convenient, but they also make it easier to consume overlapping ingredients.

For example, someone might use a multivitamin together with a bone-health supplement, an immune-support supplement and an energy product. Several of these products could contain the same vitamins or minerals.

Adding the amounts together can provide a more accurate picture of total intake.

This is particularly important for nutrients for which excessive intake from supplements or fortified foods can cause adverse effects.

21. Consider the Upper Limit for Certain Nutrients

Some nutrients have a Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL). The UL is the highest average daily intake level likely to pose no risk of adverse health effects for most people in a particular life-stage group.

The UL is not a recommended amount to consume. It is a reference point used to help assess the potential risk associated with excessive intake.

Importantly, ULs apply to total intake from food, beverages and supplements where applicable, although the specific scientific basis differs by nutrient.

This is another reason why simply choosing the product with the largest number on the label is not a good strategy.

22. Be Especially Careful With Iron

Iron is an example of a nutrient where taking a supplement without a clear reason may not be appropriate for everyone.

Iron is essential for the production of hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen throughout the body. Iron deficiency can lead to iron-deficiency anemia.

However, not everyone who feels tired or weak has an iron deficiency.

Taking iron when it is not needed can expose a person to unnecessary amounts of the mineral and can cause gastrointestinal side effects such as constipation, nausea or abdominal discomfort.

People should not assume that fatigue automatically means they need an iron supplement. If iron deficiency is suspected, appropriate medical evaluation can help determine whether testing or treatment is necessary.

23. Check Vitamin D Amounts Carefully

Vitamin D is another nutrient frequently found in supplements.

Vitamin D helps the body absorb calcium and is important for maintaining healthy bones. It also has other roles in normal body function.

Because vitamin D supplements can contain substantially different amounts, always check the amount per serving.

Vitamin D is commonly expressed in micrograms (mcg) and International Units (IU). The label may provide one or both measurements.

Do not assume that a product containing a very high amount is necessarily more appropriate for you.

24. Check Calcium and Magnesium Separately

Calcium and magnesium are commonly included in multivitamins, mineral products and specialized supplements.

When evaluating these products, look at the amount provided per serving and compare it with your overall dietary intake.

Food can provide substantial amounts of minerals. Dairy products, fortified foods, leafy vegetables, legumes, nuts, seeds and other foods can contribute calcium or magnesium depending on the food.

A supplement should therefore be considered as part of the person's overall diet rather than as an isolated product.

25. Look for Added Sugars and Sweeteners

Gummies, chewable supplements, powders and flavored liquids can contain sweeteners.

If you take these products regularly, check the ingredient information to understand what has been added to the formulation.

Some products may contain added sugars, while others may use sugar alcohols or non-sugar sweeteners.

The amount and type of sweetener may be particularly relevant for people who need to manage their overall sugar intake or who experience digestive symptoms from certain sugar alcohols.

26. Check for Allergens and Ingredients You Avoid

Always review the ingredient information if you have food allergies or follow a specific dietary pattern.

Depending on the product, ingredients such as milk, soy, wheat, eggs, fish, shellfish or other substances may be relevant.

Do not rely only on phrases such as "clean," "pure" or "hypoallergenic."

Read the actual ingredient information and, when necessary, contact the manufacturer to clarify questions about ingredients or manufacturing processes.

27. "Free From" Does Not Mean "Better"

Labels sometimes emphasize that a product is free from particular ingredients.

A "free from" statement may be useful for someone who needs to avoid a specific ingredient, but it does not automatically mean the product is more effective.

For example, a product being free from a particular color, sweetener or other ingredient does not by itself demonstrate that the active ingredients work better.

Focus first on whether the product contains the ingredients you actually need, in appropriate amounts, and whether there is reliable evidence supporting their intended use.

28. Look for Quality Information, But Understand What It Means

Some supplement manufacturers voluntarily participate in independent quality-testing or certification programs.

Depending on the program, third-party testing may examine whether a product contains the ingredients declared on the label and whether it meets particular standards for identity, purity or manufacturing quality.

Examples of organizations associated with voluntary third-party certification include USP, NSF and ConsumerLab.

However, a certification or quality seal should not be interpreted as proof that a supplement will prevent or treat a disease.

Quality testing and effectiveness are two different questions.

29. Understand the Difference Between Quality and Effectiveness

A supplement can be manufactured correctly and contain exactly what its label says while still having limited evidence for a particular health claim.

Conversely, evidence that an ingredient may have a particular effect does not mean every product containing that ingredient has the same quality.

When choosing a supplement, therefore, consider both questions:

  • Does the product contain what the label says it contains?
  • Is there reliable evidence that the ingredient and amount are useful for the intended purpose?
  • Is the product appropriate for your individual circumstances?

30. Check Where You Are Buying the Supplement

The seller matters as well as the label.

Be cautious when buying supplements from unknown websites, social media accounts or sellers offering products at unusually low prices.

Counterfeit or adulterated products can create serious health risks, particularly when the product contains undeclared pharmaceutical ingredients or other substances.

Whenever possible, buy from reputable retailers and check that the packaging is intact.

If the product looks different from previous purchases, has unusual packaging, has a damaged seal or contains information that does not match the manufacturer's official information, consider contacting the manufacturer before using it.

A good supplement choice starts with the label, but it should not end there. Consider the evidence, your diet, your medicines and your individual health needs.

Key Points

  • Always check the Supplement Facts or nutrition information panel before buying a supplement.
  • Look at the serving size and determine how much of each nutrient you actually receive per serving.
  • Check the amount of the active ingredient rather than judging a product only by the front label.
  • Review the ingredient list for additional ingredients, allergens and substances you may need to avoid.
  • More of a nutrient is not necessarily better, and some nutrients can cause harm when consumed in excessive amounts.
  • Pay attention to the form of a nutrient because different forms may have different characteristics and absorption can vary.
  • Be cautious about products making claims that a supplement can diagnose, treat, cure or prevent disease.
  • Consider whether you actually need the supplement and whether your diet, age, medications or health circumstances affect the decision.
