What Is Vitamin D and Why Is It Important?

Vitamin D is an important nutrient involved in several normal functions of the body. Learn what vitamin D is, where it comes from, how sunlight and food contribute to vitamin D intake and what to consider before using a vitamin D supplement.

Vitamin D is often called the “sunshine vitamin” because the body can produce it when skin is exposed to ultraviolet B radiation from sunlight. However, vitamin D is more than simply a nutrient associated with sunshine. It plays an important role in normal calcium and phosphorus metabolism and supports the normal development and maintenance of bones.

Vitamin D is also involved in normal muscle function and contributes to the normal function of the immune system. Because vitamin D can come from sunlight, certain foods and dietary supplements, understanding the different sources can help people make informed nutrition decisions.

What Is Vitamin D?

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble vitamin that belongs to a group of compounds involved in regulating several processes in the body. Unlike many nutrients that are obtained primarily from food, vitamin D has a distinctive relationship with sunlight because the skin can produce vitamin D when exposed to suitable ultraviolet B radiation.

Vitamin D obtained through sunlight, food or supplements goes through processes in the body that convert it into forms that can be used by different tissues. The body carefully regulates these processes because vitamin D is involved in maintaining normal levels of calcium and phosphorus.

There are different forms of vitamin D. Vitamin D2, also called ergocalciferol, is found in some foods and supplements. Vitamin D3, known as cholecalciferol, can be produced in the skin after exposure to ultraviolet B radiation and is also found in some animal-derived foods and supplements.

Why Is Vitamin D Important?

Vitamin D is important because it contributes to several normal functions in the body. One of its best-known roles is helping the body handle calcium and phosphorus, minerals that are important for the normal structure and maintenance of bones and teeth.

Vitamin D also contributes to normal muscle function. Muscles require a range of nutrients and minerals to work properly, and vitamin D is part of the body's normal nutritional system supporting muscle function.

Another important role of vitamin D is its contribution to normal immune system function. The immune system is complex and depends on many nutrients and biological processes. Vitamin D is one nutrient involved in maintaining normal immune function.

Vitamin D also contributes to normal absorption and utilization of calcium and phosphorus. This helps explain why vitamin D is closely associated with healthy bones and normal mineral balance.

Vitamin D and Bone Health

Bone is living tissue that is continuously changing. The body needs minerals such as calcium and phosphorus as part of normal bone formation and maintenance. Vitamin D helps the body absorb and use these minerals.

Adequate vitamin D is therefore an important part of overall nutritional support for normal bones. However, bone health does not depend on vitamin D alone. Calcium intake, protein, physical activity, age, genetics, overall nutrition and other factors can also influence bone health.

This is one reason it is generally better to think about vitamin D as part of an overall healthy lifestyle rather than as a single nutrient that can solve every bone-health concern.

How Does the Body Get Vitamin D?

There are three major ways people can obtain vitamin D: sunlight exposure, food and dietary supplements. The amount obtained from each source can vary widely depending on lifestyle, diet, location, season, clothing, skin exposure and other individual factors.

Sunlight is a particularly interesting source because the body can produce vitamin D in the skin after exposure to ultraviolet B radiation. This does not mean that spending as much time as possible in direct sunlight is necessary or advisable. Excessive ultraviolet exposure can damage the skin and increase the risk of skin cancer.

Vitamin D can also be obtained from certain foods. Naturally occurring dietary sources are relatively limited compared with many other nutrients, but some foods can make useful contributions to vitamin D intake. Some foods are also fortified with vitamin D.

Supplements provide another source. Vitamin D supplements are available in different strengths and formulations. Whether supplementation is appropriate depends on factors such as diet, individual needs, medical circumstances and, when clinically appropriate, blood test results.

Vitamin D From Sunlight

The body can produce vitamin D when ultraviolet B radiation reaches exposed skin. This process is one reason vitamin D is commonly associated with sunshine. However, the amount of vitamin D produced is not the same for everyone.

Geographic location, season, time of day, skin pigmentation, age, clothing and sunscreen use can all influence how much ultraviolet B radiation reaches the skin. Environmental conditions can also affect exposure.

Because these factors vary considerably, there is no single amount of sunlight that can guarantee an appropriate vitamin D level for every person.

It is also important not to treat intentional sunburn or prolonged unprotected ultraviolet exposure as a vitamin D strategy. Protecting the skin from excessive ultraviolet radiation remains an important part of responsible health behavior.

Foods That Contain Vitamin D

Only a relatively small number of foods naturally contain significant amounts of vitamin D. Some foods can nevertheless contribute to overall vitamin D intake, especially when included regularly as part of a varied eating pattern.

  • Fatty fish such as salmon, sardines and mackerel
  • Egg yolks
  • Some types of mushrooms
  • Liver in some dietary patterns
  • Foods fortified with vitamin D
  • Some fortified plant-based drinks
  • Some fortified breakfast cereals

The actual vitamin D content of a food can vary depending on the product, preparation method and whether it has been fortified. For packaged foods, checking the nutrition label can help determine how much vitamin D a particular serving provides.

Vitamin D and Fortified Foods

Food fortification means that selected nutrients are added to foods to increase their nutritional value. Vitamin D may be added to certain milk products, plant-based beverages, cereals and other foods, depending on the country and manufacturer.

Fortified foods can be especially useful for people whose diets contain few naturally occurring sources of vitamin D. However, the availability and amount of vitamin D in fortified products varies, so it is important to read the label rather than assuming that every product contains vitamin D.

What Happens When Vitamin D Intake Is Too Low?

When the body does not have enough vitamin D for its needs over time, vitamin D status can become too low. Severe deficiency can affect normal bone health and may contribute to problems such as weak or poorly mineralized bones.

Children require vitamin D as part of normal growth and bone development. Adults also require vitamin D as part of maintaining normal bones and muscles. Older adults may have additional considerations because vitamin D production in the skin can decrease with age.

Symptoms alone are not always a reliable way to know whether someone has inadequate vitamin D. A person may have few obvious symptoms even when their vitamin D status needs attention. When there is a reason to investigate vitamin D status, a healthcare professional can determine whether testing is appropriate.

Who May Need to Pay More Attention to Vitamin D?

Vitamin D needs and vitamin D status can vary between individuals. Some people may have a greater chance of having low vitamin D because of limited sunlight exposure, dietary patterns or other circumstances.

People who spend little time outdoors may have less opportunity for vitamin D production through sunlight. Individuals who cover most of their skin for cultural, occupational or personal reasons may also have reduced skin exposure to ultraviolet B radiation.

Older adults may produce vitamin D less efficiently through the skin than younger adults. People with certain conditions affecting fat absorption may also require individual medical guidance because vitamin D is a fat-soluble vitamin.

People following diets that contain few vitamin D sources may also need to pay particular attention to dietary intake. This does not automatically mean that everyone in these groups should take a high-dose supplement. Individual circumstances matter.

Vitamin D Supplements

Vitamin D supplements are widely available and can be purchased in several forms, including tablets, capsules, softgels, liquids and combination products. Some supplements contain vitamin D alone, while others combine vitamin D with calcium or other nutrients.

Vitamin D supplements can be useful when dietary intake is insufficient or when a healthcare professional recommends supplementation. However, taking more is not automatically better.

Because vitamin D is fat-soluble, excessive intake can cause the body to accumulate too much vitamin D. Very high amounts over time can lead to excessive calcium levels in the blood and potentially cause serious health problems.

This is why consumers should follow the product label and avoid combining several high-dose vitamin D products without understanding their total intake.

Vitamin D2 and Vitamin D3: What Is the Difference?

Vitamin D2 and vitamin D3 are two forms of vitamin D used in foods and supplements. Vitamin D2 is also called ergocalciferol, while vitamin D3 is called cholecalciferol.

Vitamin D3 is naturally produced in human skin after suitable ultraviolet B exposure. It can also be found in certain animal-derived foods and supplements. Vitamin D2 is associated with certain mushrooms and is also used in some fortified foods and supplements.

Both forms can contribute to vitamin D status. However, individual products vary, so consumers should read the supplement label carefully to understand which form is being provided.

Can You Get Too Much Vitamin D?

Yes. Although vitamin D is essential, excessive supplemental intake can be harmful. Vitamin D toxicity is generally associated with very high amounts, particularly from supplements rather than ordinary sunlight exposure.

Excess vitamin D can increase calcium absorption and may result in abnormally high calcium levels. Depending on severity, this can cause symptoms and complications that require medical attention.

This is an important reminder that vitamins and minerals are biologically active substances. The fact that a nutrient is necessary does not mean that larger quantities are always beneficial.

How Do You Know If You Need More Vitamin D?

It is not always possible to determine vitamin D status based on how you feel. Some people with low vitamin D may not notice obvious symptoms, while others may have health concerns that have many possible causes.

If a person has a reason to be concerned about vitamin D status, a healthcare professional can assess the situation. In appropriate circumstances, a blood test can be used to measure a marker of vitamin D status.

Testing is not necessarily required for every healthy person. The decision depends on individual health circumstances, risk factors, symptoms and professional judgment.

Vitamin D and a Balanced Diet

Vitamin D is only one part of good nutrition. A varied diet provides many nutrients that work together to support normal body functions.

Foods containing vitamin D can be combined with vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, protein sources and other nutritious foods. A balanced eating pattern can provide nutrients such as calcium, magnesium, protein and other vitamins and minerals that are relevant to overall health.

This broader approach is generally more useful than focusing on one nutrient in isolation. Nutrition is about patterns of eating rather than a single food, vitamin or supplement.

Vitamin D, Calcium and Bone Nutrition

Vitamin D and calcium are frequently discussed together because they have related roles in normal bone health. Calcium is a major mineral found in bones, while vitamin D contributes to the normal absorption and utilization of calcium.

Good dietary sources of calcium include foods such as dairy products, calcium-fortified alternatives, certain leafy vegetables, tofu prepared with calcium and some fish eaten with their edible bones.

Getting enough vitamin D while having an inadequate overall diet is not a complete approach to bone health. Nutritional needs are interconnected, which is why a balanced diet remains important.

Vitamin D and Healthy Lifestyle Habits

Maintaining healthy vitamin D status should be viewed alongside other lifestyle habits. Regular physical activity, nutritious food choices, adequate sleep, avoiding tobacco and responsible alcohol use can all contribute to overall health.

Weight-bearing and muscle-strengthening activities are particularly relevant to maintaining strong bones and muscles. The right type and amount of exercise depends on age, fitness level and individual health.

Vitamin D cannot replace physical activity, a varied diet or appropriate medical care. Instead, it is one component of a broader approach to health.

Does Sunlight Always Provide Enough Vitamin D?

Not necessarily. The amount of vitamin D produced from sunlight differs significantly between people and circumstances.

Season, latitude, weather conditions, time spent outdoors, clothing, skin pigmentation, age and other factors can affect vitamin D production. Sunscreen also reduces ultraviolet radiation reaching the skin, which is one of its intended protective effects.

For this reason, simply assuming that someone gets enough vitamin D because they live in a sunny climate is not always accurate. Similarly, deliberately exposing the skin to excessive sunlight is not a recommended substitute for responsible nutrition and medical guidance.

What Should You Look for in a Vitamin D Supplement?

If you are considering a vitamin D supplement, start by checking the label. Look for the form of vitamin D, amount per serving, serving instructions, ingredients and manufacturer information.

Avoid assuming that a more expensive product is automatically better. The most important consideration is whether the product is appropriate for your needs and whether it is manufactured and labelled responsibly.

It is also useful to consider the total amount of vitamin D you may receive from multiple products. Multivitamins, bone-health products and individual vitamin D supplements may all contain vitamin D.

Adding their amounts together without checking the labels can unintentionally result in excessive intake.

When Should You Speak With a Healthcare Professional?

Professional advice can be particularly important if you have been diagnosed with a medical condition, take prescription medication, have problems with nutrient absorption, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or are considering a high-dose vitamin D supplement.

Children and older adults may also have different nutritional considerations. A healthcare professional can help determine whether supplementation is appropriate and, when necessary, whether testing should be considered.

If a supplement has been recommended to you, follow the recommended dose rather than increasing it on your own. If you are unsure about combining products, discuss the labels with a qualified healthcare professional.

Vitamin D is important, but good health is built from many connected habits rather than one nutrient alone.

Vitamin D: The Big Picture

Vitamin D is an important fat-soluble vitamin that contributes to normal calcium and phosphorus metabolism, normal bone maintenance, normal muscle function and normal immune system function.

The body can obtain vitamin D through sunlight exposure, foods and supplements. However, the amount obtained from these sources can vary considerably. Some people may obtain adequate vitamin D through their normal lifestyle, while others may need to pay closer attention to dietary intake or discuss supplementation with a healthcare professional.

The goal should not be to obtain the maximum possible amount of vitamin D. The goal is appropriate intake for individual needs.

A balanced diet, sensible lifestyle habits and responsible use of supplements can all play a role in maintaining good nutrition. When questions arise about vitamin D deficiency, testing, supplementation or interactions with medications, professional advice is the safest way to make an individualized decision.

Key Points

  • Vitamin D is a fat-soluble vitamin involved in several normal body functions.
  • Vitamin D contributes to normal calcium and phosphorus metabolism.
  • Vitamin D supports the normal maintenance of bones and normal muscle function.
  • Vitamin D contributes to normal immune system function.
  • Sunlight can help the body produce vitamin D.
  • Certain foods and fortified products can contribute vitamin D.
  • Vitamin D supplements are available in several forms and strengths.
  • More vitamin D is not automatically better.
  • Excessive supplemental vitamin D can be harmful.
  • People with specific health circumstances may benefit from professional guidance.
