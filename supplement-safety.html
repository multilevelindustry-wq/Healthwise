When Should You Talk to a Healthcare Professional About Supplements?

Dietary supplements can provide vitamins, minerals and other substances, but they are not automatically safe simply because they are sold as supplements. Learn when speaking with a doctor, pharmacist, dietitian or another qualified healthcare professional is especially important before starting or changing a supplement.

Vitamins, minerals, herbal products and other dietary supplements are widely used by people who want to support their nutrition, address a nutrient deficiency, improve a particular aspect of health or simply add something extra to their daily routine.

But an important question is often overlooked: When should you talk to a healthcare professional before taking a supplement?

The answer is more often than many people realize. A supplement may contain ingredients that have real biological effects. Some can interact with prescription medicines or over-the-counter drugs, affect how a medicine is absorbed or processed, interfere with certain laboratory tests, or create additional risks around surgery.

This does not mean that dietary supplements are inherently dangerous. Some supplements can be useful when there is a genuine nutritional need or when a healthcare professional recommends them. The important point is that the right product, dose and duration depend on the individual.

Why Should You Talk to a Healthcare Professional About Supplements?

A dietary supplement is not simply a food in a capsule. Depending on the ingredients and dose, a supplement can affect processes in the body.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration advises consumers to talk with a healthcare professional before using dietary supplements because some supplements can interact with medicines, affect medical conditions, interfere with laboratory tests or create problems around surgery.

A healthcare professional can also help determine whether you actually need a particular supplement. In some situations, the better approach may be improving dietary intake, correcting an identified deficiency or treating an underlying medical problem rather than taking a high-dose supplement.

Your healthcare professional does not necessarily have to be a doctor. Depending on the question, a pharmacist, registered dietitian or another qualified healthcare professional may be able to help you understand possible benefits, risks and interactions.

You Should Talk to a Professional If You Take Prescription Medicines

One of the most important reasons to discuss a supplement with a healthcare professional is medication use.

Some dietary supplements can change how medicines are absorbed, metabolized or eliminated from the body. This can potentially make a medicine stronger, weaker or behave differently than expected.

This is particularly important if you take medicines regularly or take several different medications.

Do not assume that a supplement is safe simply because it is labeled "natural." Plants and concentrated botanical ingredients can contain biologically active substances.

For example, the FDA notes that St. John's wort can interact with several types of medicines, while vitamin K can interfere with the action of the blood-thinning medicine warfarin.

This is why you should tell your doctor or pharmacist about all vitamins, minerals, herbal products and other supplements you use, including products you only take occasionally.

Natural does not automatically mean risk-free. The important question is whether a supplement is appropriate for you, at the dose you are considering, alongside everything else you take.

Talk to a Healthcare Professional If You Take Several Supplements

Taking multiple supplements can make it difficult to know how much of a particular nutrient or ingredient you are actually consuming.

For example, a person might take a multivitamin, a separate mineral supplement, a sports product and an herbal product without realizing that some of the products contain overlapping ingredients.

Talk to a Healthcare Professional If You Are Breastfeeding

Breastfeeding is another situation in which supplement decisions deserve careful attention. Some substances taken by the mother can pass into breast milk, although the amount and potential effect can vary depending on the substance, dose and timing.

This does not mean that breastfeeding mothers should avoid all supplements. Some nutrients may be recommended when dietary intake is inadequate or when a healthcare professional identifies a nutritional need.

However, herbal products, concentrated botanical ingredients and high-dose supplements deserve particular caution because safety information during breastfeeding may be limited for some ingredients.

If you are breastfeeding, tell your doctor, pharmacist or another qualified healthcare professional about every supplement you are considering. This is especially important if your baby was born prematurely, has a medical condition or takes medication.

Children and Teenagers May Need Professional Guidance

Supplements should not automatically be given to children simply because a product is marketed for adults or because an ingredient is considered healthy.

Children have different nutritional requirements from adults, and the appropriate amount of a nutrient can depend on age and other factors.

Some supplements can also contain amounts that are too high for a child. This is one reason parents and caregivers should speak with a pediatrician, pharmacist or another qualified healthcare professional before giving children supplements that are not specifically recommended for them.

Extra caution is appropriate with products marketed for weight loss, bodybuilding, energy enhancement or rapid physical development. Products in these categories may contain multiple ingredients, and some may have limited safety information.

If a child has symptoms such as persistent tiredness, poor appetite, weakness, unusual weight changes or difficulty concentrating, it is better to investigate the possible cause rather than automatically assuming that a supplement is needed.

Older Adults Should Review Supplements Carefully

Older adults may have additional reasons to review supplements with a healthcare professional.

Many older adults take several prescription or over-the-counter medicines. Adding supplements to an already complex medication routine can increase the possibility of interactions.

Changes in kidney or liver function can also affect how certain substances are handled by the body. In addition, some nutrients can accumulate when the body's ability to eliminate them is reduced.

This is why a supplement that is appropriate for one person may not be appropriate for another person of the same age.

If you are an older adult, or you are helping an older family member manage medicines and supplements, keeping an up-to-date list of everything being taken can make medication and supplement reviews much safer.

Talk to a Professional If You Have a Medical Condition

People with certain medical conditions may need to be especially careful about supplements.

Conditions affecting the kidneys or liver are important examples because these organs play major roles in processing and eliminating many substances.

People with heart conditions, bleeding disorders, diabetes, high blood pressure or other chronic conditions should also discuss new supplements with their healthcare professional when appropriate.

The reason is not that every supplement is dangerous for people with these conditions. Rather, the effects of a supplement can depend on the person's medications, nutritional status, organ function and specific health circumstances.

For example, some supplements can influence blood glucose levels. Others may affect blood pressure or blood clotting. These effects can become more important when a person is already taking medication for one of these conditions.

If you have a diagnosed medical condition, do not use a supplement as a substitute for prescribed treatment without discussing the decision with your healthcare professional.

Kidney Disease Is an Important Reason to Ask First

People with kidney disease should be particularly cautious about starting supplements without professional advice.

The kidneys help remove waste products and regulate levels of many substances in the body. When kidney function is reduced, certain substances may behave differently or accumulate.

Some mineral supplements can therefore require special consideration in people with impaired kidney function.

If you have chronic kidney disease, are receiving dialysis or have been told that your kidney function is reduced, speak with your healthcare professional before starting vitamins, minerals, herbal products or other supplements.

Liver Disease Also Warrants Extra Caution

The liver is involved in processing many substances, including medicines and some compounds found in supplements.

Some herbal and dietary supplement products have been associated with liver injury. The risk can depend on the ingredient, dose, product quality and individual circumstances.

If you have liver disease or a history of abnormal liver tests, discuss supplements with a healthcare professional before starting them.

This is particularly important if a product contains several herbal ingredients or is marketed for rapid weight loss, bodybuilding, detoxification or performance enhancement.

Ask Before Taking Supplements Around Surgery

If you are preparing for surgery or another medical procedure, tell your healthcare team about the supplements you take.

Some supplements may affect bleeding, blood pressure, blood glucose or the effects of medicines used during a procedure.

Herbal products are particularly important to disclose because their effects can vary substantially depending on the ingredients.

Do not decide on your own that a supplement is safe to continue simply because it is sold without a prescription.

Your surgeon, anesthesiologist, pharmacist or other healthcare professional can tell you whether a product should be stopped before a procedure and when it may be appropriate to restart it.

Before surgery, your healthcare team needs the complete picture—not only your prescription medicines, but also vitamins, minerals, herbal products and other supplements.

Talk to a Professional If You Develop Side Effects

You should not ignore new or unexplained symptoms after starting a supplement.

Possible adverse effects can vary widely depending on the product. Some people may experience digestive symptoms such as nausea, abdominal discomfort or diarrhea. Other reactions can involve the skin, nervous system, heart or other parts of the body.

An allergic reaction can also occur. Symptoms such as swelling of the lips, tongue or throat, difficulty breathing, fainting or severe dizziness require urgent medical attention.

Less dramatic symptoms can still be worth discussing with a healthcare professional, particularly if they begin soon after a new supplement is introduced or repeatedly occur after taking it.

Do not simply increase the dose because you believe the supplement is not working. A healthcare professional can help determine whether the symptoms could be related to the product or whether another explanation needs to be investigated.

Be Careful With Supplements That Promise Rapid Results

You should consider speaking with a healthcare professional before using products that promise dramatic or unusually rapid results.

Be particularly cautious with products marketed as "fat burners," "detoxes," "hormone boosters," "testosterone boosters," "miracle cures" or products promising major weight loss in a short period.

Marketing language is not the same thing as clinical evidence. A product can be promoted aggressively without having strong evidence that it provides the advertised benefit.

Some products may also contain numerous ingredients, making it difficult for consumers to understand which ingredient is responsible for an effect or side effect.

If a supplement promises to treat or cure a disease, that is another reason to step back and seek reliable medical advice rather than replacing proven treatment with the product.

Talk to a Professional If You Are Taking Blood Thinners

People taking anticoagulant or antiplatelet medicines should be particularly careful about starting new supplements.

Some supplements can affect blood clotting or interact with medicines used to prevent blood clots.

Vitamin K is an important example for people taking warfarin. Changes in vitamin K intake can affect warfarin treatment, which is why people taking warfarin are generally advised to discuss significant dietary or supplement changes with their healthcare professional.

Other supplements may also have effects relevant to bleeding or clotting. Because the consequences of an interaction can be serious, do not assume that an herbal product or vitamin is harmless simply because it does not require a prescription.

Talk to a Professional If You Take Diabetes Medicines

People taking medicines for diabetes should also discuss new supplements with a healthcare professional.

Some supplements may influence blood glucose levels. When combined with diabetes medicines, this could potentially contribute to blood glucose becoming too low or otherwise alter glucose management.

A supplement should not be used to replace prescribed diabetes treatment unless a qualified healthcare professional specifically recommends a change.

When Supplements May Need Extra Caution

Supplements are not automatically safe simply because they are sold without a prescription. Some vitamins, minerals, herbs and other dietary ingredients can have strong effects on the body. The level of risk can depend on the dose, the person's health, the product ingredients and whether other medicines or supplements are being used at the same time.

Extra caution is appropriate when a supplement is marketed as a treatment for a disease, promises rapid results or recommends unusually high doses. Claims such as "detoxes the body," "cures disease," "melts fat" or "replaces medication" should not be treated as proof that a product is safe or effective.

A healthcare professional can help determine whether the ingredient is appropriate for your circumstances and whether there is a better supported option.

If You Take Prescription or Over-the-Counter Medicines

One of the most important reasons to discuss supplements with a healthcare professional is the possibility of interactions with medicines. Some supplements can change how a medicine is absorbed, broken down or eliminated. Others may increase or decrease a medicine's effects.

For example, vitamin K can affect the action of warfarin, an anticoagulant medicine. St. John's wort can interact with a number of medicines because it can change how certain drugs are metabolized. Some supplements may also increase bleeding risk when combined with medicines that affect blood clotting.

This does not mean that every person taking these medicines must avoid all supplements. The important point is that the combination should be reviewed by a qualified healthcare professional who can consider the specific medicine, dose and supplement.

Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Pregnancy and breastfeeding are important times to review supplement use with a healthcare professional. Nutrient requirements change during pregnancy, but taking more of a nutrient is not necessarily better.

Some supplements contain ingredients that have not been adequately studied during pregnancy or breastfeeding. Herbal products can be particularly difficult to evaluate because products may contain multiple active ingredients and evidence about their safety may be limited.

People who are pregnant, planning a pregnancy or breastfeeding should tell their doctor, midwife, pharmacist or other qualified healthcare professional about all supplements they are considering.

This is especially important if a product is marketed for weight loss, hormone balance, energy, sexual performance, bodybuilding or detoxification.

Supplements and Children

Children are not simply small adults. Their nutritional needs, body size and ability to process substances can differ from those of adults. A supplement that is appropriate for an adult may not be appropriate for a child.

Parents and caregivers should speak with a pediatrician, pharmacist or another qualified healthcare professional before giving a child a supplement that is not already part of an advised treatment plan.

This is particularly important for products containing concentrated vitamins, minerals, stimulants, herbal ingredients or multiple active ingredients.

If You Have a Chronic Health Condition

People living with a chronic medical condition may need additional care when choosing supplements. Conditions involving the kidneys, liver, heart, blood clotting, thyroid or digestive system can affect how nutrients and other substances are handled by the body.

For example, people with reduced kidney function may need to limit or carefully manage certain minerals and other substances. Taking large amounts of particular nutrients without professional guidance may create unnecessary risks.

If you have a diagnosed medical condition, do not assume that a supplement advertised for general wellness is appropriate for you. Tell your healthcare professional about the exact product, ingredients and dose before starting it.

If You Are Having Symptoms

A supplement should not be used as a substitute for finding the cause of unexplained symptoms. Persistent tiredness, weakness, dizziness, unexplained weight changes, hair loss, digestive problems or changes in appetite can have many possible causes.

For example, tiredness can be associated with poor sleep, stress, infections, anemia, thyroid disorders, medication effects and many other conditions. Taking an iron supplement simply because you feel tired could delay appropriate evaluation and may expose you to unnecessary iron.

If symptoms are new, persistent, severe or getting worse, speak with an appropriate healthcare professional rather than trying to identify the cause through supplements alone.

Before Taking High-Dose Vitamins or Minerals

Vitamins and minerals are essential nutrients, but the body does not necessarily benefit from taking very large amounts. Some nutrients have established upper intake levels because excessive intake can cause adverse effects.

Fat-soluble vitamins such as vitamins A, D, E and K can be stored in the body. Excessive intake can therefore be different from taking too much of a water-soluble vitamin that is more readily eliminated.

Minerals can also become harmful at excessive levels. Iron, for example, can be dangerous in large amounts, particularly when it is taken unnecessarily or accidentally by children.

Before using a high-dose product, ask a healthcare professional whether the dose is appropriate and whether testing or monitoring is needed.

Be Careful With Multiple Supplements

Taking several supplements at the same time can make it difficult to know how much of a particular nutrient you are actually consuming.

A multivitamin may already contain vitamin D, zinc, vitamin B6, vitamin A or other nutrients. Adding separate products containing the same ingredients can substantially increase the total daily intake.

This is one reason it is useful to keep a complete list of everything you take. Include prescription medicines, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, minerals, herbal products, protein products and other dietary supplements.

What to Bring to Your Appointment

If you are asking a healthcare professional whether a supplement is suitable for you, bring the product information whenever possible. A photograph of the label can also be useful.

  • The exact product name
  • A photo or copy of the Supplement Facts or ingredient label
  • The amount you take each day
  • How often you take it
  • The reason you started taking it
  • Other medicines you use
  • Other supplements you use
  • Any symptoms or side effects you have noticed
The safest supplement decision starts with knowing exactly what you are taking, why you are taking it, and whether it fits your individual health needs.

When a Pharmacist Can Help

A pharmacist can be a useful source of information when you have questions about supplements and medicines. Pharmacists are trained to understand medication effects, dosing and potential interactions.

If you are unsure whether a supplement could interact with a prescription or over-the-counter medicine, asking a pharmacist can be an appropriate first step. They can also advise you about whether you should contact your doctor or another healthcare professional for further assessment.

Questions Worth Asking Before Starting a Supplement

You do not need to wait until you experience a problem before asking questions. A short conversation with a healthcare professional can help you make a more informed decision.

  • Do I actually need this supplement?
  • Is there evidence that it can help with my goal?
  • Is the dose appropriate for me?
  • Could it interact with any of my medicines?
  • Could another supplement I take contain the same ingredient?
  • Are there side effects I should watch for?
  • Should I stop taking it before a medical procedure?
  • Do I need a blood test or other evaluation?

Supplements Before Surgery or Medical Procedures

Tell your healthcare team about supplements before surgery or another medical procedure. Some supplements may affect bleeding, blood pressure, heart rate, sedation or the way medicines work.

Do not assume that a supplement is harmless because it is labelled "natural." The term natural does not tell you whether an ingredient can affect the body or interact with medication.

Your healthcare team can tell you whether a particular supplement should be stopped before a procedure and when it is appropriate to restart it.

The Difference Between a Nutrient Deficiency and a Supplement Trend

A nutrient deficiency is a medical or nutritional problem that may require an appropriate dietary change, supplementation or treatment of an underlying cause. A supplement trend, on the other hand, may be based largely on advertising, social media or personal testimonials.

If a supplement is being promoted as something that everyone needs, consider whether there is evidence supporting that claim. Nutritional needs vary between people, and more of a nutrient is not automatically better.

When a deficiency is suspected, a healthcare professional can help determine whether symptoms, diet, medical history and appropriate laboratory testing support that possibility.

When a Supplement Causes a Side Effect

If you develop a new or unexpected symptom after starting a supplement, do not automatically assume that the symptom is unrelated. Depending on the ingredient and the circumstances, supplements can cause side effects or interact with medicines.

Common problems can include nausea, stomach discomfort, diarrhea, constipation, headache or changes in how a person feels. More serious reactions can also occur with some products or ingredients.

If a symptom is severe, rapidly worsening or associated with difficulty breathing, swelling, fainting, severe confusion or other signs of a serious reaction, seek urgent medical attention rather than waiting for a supplement review.

When You Should Stop Self-Treating With Supplements

Supplements should not become a substitute for medical evaluation when a health problem continues. If you have been taking a product for a specific symptom and the symptom does not improve, it is reasonable to reconsider the approach.

Continuing to increase the dose because the expected result has not occurred can increase risk without addressing the underlying problem.

A healthcare professional can help determine whether the symptom requires an examination, laboratory testing, medication review, dietary assessment or another form of evaluation.

How to Think About Supplement Safety

A useful way to approach supplements is to consider three questions: Do you need it? Is there evidence that it can help with your particular goal? And is it safe for you at the dose you plan to take?

These questions are more useful than simply asking whether a product is natural. A product can contain naturally occurring ingredients and still have biological effects, side effects or interactions.

It is also important to distinguish between correcting a documented deficiency and taking a supplement for general health without a specific need. In some circumstances supplementation can be valuable, but the right choice depends on the individual.

A Healthcare Professional Can Help You Make a Personalized Decision

One of the biggest advantages of discussing supplements with a healthcare professional is personalization. General information on the internet cannot account for every person's medical history, medication list, nutritional intake, allergies and other circumstances.

A doctor, pharmacist, registered dietitian or another appropriately qualified professional may be able to help you decide whether a supplement is appropriate, how it fits into your overall nutrition and whether monitoring is necessary.

This does not mean that every supplement requires a doctor's appointment. For many healthy adults, basic nutrition advice may be enough. However, the more complicated the situation becomes, the more useful professional guidance can be.

What About Supplements Recommended Online?

Social media, blogs and online videos can introduce people to useful nutrition information, but online recommendations should not automatically be treated as personalized medical advice.

Personal testimonials can describe one person's experience, but they do not establish that a product will work for everyone. A supplement may also be presented online at a dose or for a purpose that is not appropriate for another person.

Before following an online recommendation, check the ingredients, intended use, dose and available evidence. If you have a medical condition or take medication, discuss the product with a healthcare professional.

How to Choose a Supplement More Carefully

If you and your healthcare professional decide that a supplement is appropriate, read the label carefully. Look at the serving size, amount of each ingredient, directions and any warnings.

Avoid assuming that a higher dose is more effective. The appropriate amount depends on the nutrient or ingredient and the reason it is being used.

Be especially cautious with products containing many ingredients, proprietary blends or claims that sound too good to be true. A long ingredient list does not automatically mean that a product is better.

Keep Your Healthcare Professional Updated

Supplement use should be included when you discuss your health with your healthcare team. People sometimes mention prescription medicines but forget to mention vitamins, herbal products or other supplements.

Keeping an updated list on your phone can make this easier. Include the product name, dose and how often you take it. Update the list whenever you start, stop or change a supplement.

This simple habit can help healthcare professionals make safer decisions about medicines, tests, procedures and treatment.

When Supplement Questions Become Medical Questions

Sometimes the question "Which supplement should I take?" is actually a different question. It may be "Why am I tired?", "Why is my hair falling out?", "Why is my blood test abnormal?" or "Why do I have persistent digestive symptoms?"

In these situations, choosing a supplement before identifying the cause may not be the best approach. Some symptoms have many possible explanations, and treatment should be based on the underlying problem when possible.

If you are repeatedly searching for supplements to manage a persistent symptom, consider speaking with a healthcare professional instead of continuing to experiment with products.

The Bottom Line

Supplements can play a useful role in nutrition and healthcare when they are appropriate for an individual's needs. They are not, however, automatically necessary or risk-free.

You should consider speaking with a healthcare professional before starting a supplement if you take prescription or over-the-counter medicines, are pregnant or breastfeeding, are planning surgery, have a chronic medical condition, are considering high-dose vitamins or minerals, are giving supplements to a child, or are experiencing unexplained or persistent symptoms.

Professional advice is also valuable when you are taking several supplements at once, when a product causes unexpected symptoms, or when you are considering a supplement marketed as a treatment for a medical condition.

For healthy adults, many nutritional needs can be met through a varied diet. When supplementation is needed, the goal should be to use the right product, at an appropriate dose, for a clear reason.

The safest approach is not to fear supplements or to assume that every supplement is beneficial. Instead, make decisions based on reliable evidence, your individual circumstances and advice from an appropriate healthcare professional when needed.

Key Points

  • Supplements can have side effects and can interact with medicines.
  • Speak with a healthcare professional if you take prescription or over-the-counter medicines.
  • Pregnancy, breastfeeding and childhood are situations where supplement use deserves particular care.
  • People with chronic medical conditions should discuss appropriate supplements with a qualified professional.
  • Persistent or unexplained symptoms should not simply be treated with supplements without considering the underlying cause.
  • High-dose vitamins and minerals can create health risks when taken unnecessarily.
  • Taking several supplements can unintentionally increase total intake of certain nutrients.
  • Always tell your healthcare team about the supplements you use, especially before surgery or medical procedures.
