What Is Omega-3? Benefits, Food Sources and Supplement Guide

Omega-3 fatty acids are essential fats that play important roles in the body. Learn about ALA, EPA and DHA, the foods that provide omega-3s, their relationship with heart and brain health, and what to consider before choosing an omega-3 supplement.

Omega-3 is a term you may have seen on food labels, fish-oil supplements and nutrition articles. But omega-3 is not one single substance. It refers to a family of polyunsaturated fatty acids that have different roles in the body and come from different foods.

The three omega-3 fatty acids most commonly discussed in human nutrition are alpha-linolenic acid, or ALA, eicosapentaenoic acid, or EPA, and docosahexaenoic acid, or DHA. ALA is found mainly in plant foods and oils, while EPA and DHA are found primarily in fish, seafood and certain algae-based products.

What Is Omega-3?

Omega-3 fatty acids are a group of dietary fats. They are called polyunsaturated fats because of their chemical structure. Fat is not simply something the body tries to eliminate. Certain fats are essential nutrients that the body needs for normal functions.

ALA is considered an essential fatty acid because the human body cannot make it in sufficient amounts. This means it needs to be obtained from food or another dietary source. The body can convert some ALA into EPA and DHA, but this conversion is limited.

EPA and DHA therefore have a different nutritional situation. They can be obtained directly from foods such as fatty fish and seafood. DHA is also found in high concentrations in certain tissues, including the brain and retina.

The Three Main Omega-3 Fatty Acids

Understanding the difference between ALA, EPA and DHA is important because products labelled "omega-3" can contain very different amounts and types of these fats.

Alpha-Linolenic Acid (ALA)

ALA is the main omega-3 fatty acid found in plant foods. Sources include flaxseed and flaxseed oil, chia seeds, walnuts, soybean oil and canola oil.

ALA is essential because the body cannot produce it on its own. However, eating ALA does not provide the same amount of EPA and DHA that would be obtained by consuming foods that directly contain EPA and DHA.

Eicosapentaenoic Acid (EPA)

EPA is a long-chain omega-3 fatty acid found mainly in fish and seafood. EPA is involved in normal cellular processes and is particularly important when discussing the relationship between omega-3 intake and cardiovascular health.

Docosahexaenoic Acid (DHA)

DHA is another long-chain omega-3 fatty acid. It is an important structural component of cell membranes, with particularly high levels in the brain and retina.

DHA is especially important during periods of rapid growth and development, including pregnancy and early childhood. This is one reason fish and seafood are included in dietary guidance for pregnant people, provided lower-mercury choices are selected.

Why Does the Body Need Omega-3?

Omega-3 fatty acids are components of the membranes surrounding cells throughout the body. They also participate in the production of signalling molecules involved in normal physiological processes.

DHA is particularly concentrated in the brain and retina. EPA and DHA are also involved in normal cardiovascular and immune-related processes.

This does not mean that taking more omega-3 will automatically make a person healthier. Nutrition is more complicated than taking a single nutrient. Overall dietary patterns, existing health conditions, medications, physical activity and other lifestyle factors all matter.

Omega-3 is not one single nutrient. ALA, EPA and DHA are different fatty acids with different food sources and biological roles.

Omega-3 Food Sources

One of the most practical ways to obtain omega-3 fatty acids is through food. Different foods provide different types of omega-3, so variety can be useful.

  • Salmon
  • Sardines
  • Herring
  • Mackerel
  • Trout
  • Anchovies
  • Oysters and other seafood
  • Flaxseed and flaxseed oil
  • Chia seeds
  • Walnuts
  • Soybean and canola oils

Omega-3 and Brain Health

DHA is an important structural component of the brain. Because of this, omega-3 and brain health have been the subject of extensive scientific research.

Having adequate nutrients during pregnancy and childhood is particularly important because the brain and nervous system undergo substantial development during these periods. Fish and seafood provide DHA and EPA together with protein, vitamin B12, vitamin D, selenium and other nutrients.

However, claims that omega-3 supplements can automatically improve memory, intelligence or prevent dementia should be treated cautiously. Research on omega-3 supplements and cognitive outcomes has produced mixed results, and a supplement should not be presented as a guaranteed treatment for memory problems or neurological disease.

Omega-3 During Pregnancy

Pregnancy is one area where omega-3 nutrition receives particular attention. DHA is important for normal development of the fetal brain and eyes, and fish provides DHA and EPA along with other valuable nutrients.

The important point is that pregnancy does not mean avoiding all fish. Current FDA and EPA guidance recommends that people who are pregnant or breastfeeding choose a variety of fish that are lower in mercury.

FDA guidance recommends 8 to 12 ounces of a variety of lower-mercury fish per week for people who are pregnant or breastfeeding. Certain large fish with higher mercury concentrations should be avoided.

Examples of fish that are among the FDA's fish-to- avoid list for pregnancy include king mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, shark, swordfish, tilefish from the Gulf of Mexico and bigeye tuna.

If you are pregnant, trying to become pregnant or breastfeeding, choose seafood according to current local or national food-safety guidance and discuss supplements with your healthcare professional.

Does Omega-3 Lower Triglycerides?

This is one of the more established areas of omega-3 research. EPA and DHA can reduce blood triglyceride concentrations, particularly when used at appropriate doses.

However, the dose matters. The amounts of EPA and DHA found in an ordinary food supplement may be very different from doses used in clinical treatment.

People with very high triglycerides should not simply purchase a high-dose fish-oil product and assume it is equivalent to medical treatment. Healthcare professionals can determine whether prescription therapy, dietary changes, other medication or a combination of approaches is appropriate.

What Is Fish Oil?

Fish oil is an oil obtained from fish tissue. It commonly contains EPA and DHA, the two long-chain omega-3 fatty acids most often associated with fish and seafood.

Fish-oil supplements can be sold in different formulations. The amount of total fish oil shown on the front of a bottle is not necessarily the same as the amount of EPA plus DHA.

For example, a product may contain a particular amount of fish oil while providing a smaller combined amount of EPA and DHA. This is why reading the Supplement Facts panel is more useful than looking only at the large number printed on the front of the package.

Fish Oil vs. Algae Oil

Algae-based omega-3 products are another option. Certain algae are sources of DHA, and some products also provide EPA.

Algae oil can be useful for people who do not eat fish or seafood. It is also an important source of omega-3 for some vegetarian and vegan diets, depending on the specific product.

The nutritional value of an algae product depends on which fatty acids it contains and the amount provided per serving. Therefore, consumers should check whether the product contains DHA, EPA or both.

Is Omega-3 the Same as Cod Liver Oil?

No. Cod liver oil is a type of fish oil obtained from cod liver. It contains omega-3 fatty acids, but it can also provide vitamins A and D.

This difference matters because vitamin A can be harmful in excessive amounts. A person should not assume that all fish-oil products have the same nutritional profile.

Always check the complete product label, especially if you are already taking multivitamins or other supplements containing vitamins A or D.

How Much Omega-3 Do You Need?

There is no single daily omega-3 number that applies equally to every person and every form of omega-3. Recommendations may be expressed separately for ALA, EPA and DHA, and needs can vary according to age, sex, pregnancy status and other factors.

In the United States, adequate intake values have traditionally been established for ALA rather than for EPA and DHA. For adults, the amount of ALA recommended varies by sex, with different amounts also applying during pregnancy and breastfeeding.

This is one reason it is misleading when a supplement advertisement claims that everyone needs a specific large dose of "omega-3."

Can You Get Enough Omega-3 From Food?

Many people can obtain omega-3 fatty acids through a balanced diet. Plant foods can provide ALA, while fish and seafood provide EPA and DHA.

The American Heart Association recommends eating two servings of fish per week, particularly fatty fish, as part of a heart-healthy eating pattern.

Eating fish also provides nutrients that are not limited to omega-3 fatty acids. Fish can contribute protein, vitamin B12, vitamin D, selenium and other nutrients depending on the species.

For people who do not eat fish, plant sources of ALA and algae-derived DHA or EPA products may be alternatives, depending on dietary preferences and nutritional needs.

A supplement can provide a nutrient, but it cannot replace the full nutritional value of a varied diet.

Omega-3 Supplements: What Should You Look For?

If you are considering an omega-3 supplement, start by reading the Supplement Facts panel rather than relying on advertising claims.

  • Check the amount of EPA per serving.
  • Check the amount of DHA per serving.
  • Check the suggested serving size.
  • Look at the complete ingredient list.
  • Check the expiration or best-before information.
  • Look for appropriate quality testing when available.
  • Avoid assuming that a larger capsule means a higher EPA and DHA content.

The actual amount of EPA and DHA is generally more informative than the total amount of oil listed in large print on the package.

Are Omega-3 Supplements Safe?

Omega-3 supplements are generally well tolerated when used appropriately, but "natural" does not automatically mean risk-free.

Common side effects reported with fish-oil supplements can include an unpleasant taste or aftertaste, bad breath, heartburn, nausea and gastrointestinal discomfort.

Higher doses deserve more caution. Omega-3 fatty acids can affect blood clotting, and people taking medicines that affect clotting or bleeding should discuss omega-3 supplementation with a healthcare professional.

People preparing for surgery should also tell their healthcare team about all supplements they take. This allows the healthcare professional to consider the complete picture, including medications and supplements.

Can Omega-3 Interact With Medication?

Supplements can interact with medicines, and omega-3 products are no exception.

People taking anticoagulant or antiplatelet medicines, or medicines that affect blood clotting, should ask their healthcare professional before starting a new omega-3 supplement.

This is especially important when considering concentrated or high-dose products. Do not stop or change prescribed medication simply because you have read that omega-3 may have health benefits.

If you take prescription medicines regularly, bring the supplement label to your pharmacist or doctor so they can evaluate the exact product and dose.

Does Omega-3 Prevent Heart Disease?

Omega-3 has a strong reputation for heart health, but the evidence needs to be understood carefully.

Eating fish as part of a healthy dietary pattern is associated with cardiovascular benefits. Fatty fish provide EPA and DHA as well as other nutrients.

However, research on omega-3 supplements for preventing cardiovascular events in people who already have or are at risk of cardiovascular disease is more complicated.

Some people may benefit from specific prescription omega-3 treatments under medical supervision, particularly for very high triglyceride levels. These treatments should not be confused with ordinary dietary supplements.

Therefore, it is more accurate to say that omega-3 fatty acids are important nutrients and that fish consumption is part of a heart-healthy dietary pattern than to claim that taking a fish-oil capsule guarantees protection from heart disease.

Omega-3 and Inflammation

Omega-3 fatty acids are involved in biological processes related to inflammation and immune signalling. This has led to extensive research into their possible role in inflammatory conditions.

However, the phrase "anti-inflammatory supplement" can be misleading when it is used as a blanket medical claim.

Omega-3 should not be considered a replacement for prescribed treatment for inflammatory diseases. Anyone with a diagnosed medical condition should discuss supplement use with an appropriate healthcare professional.

Omega-3 and Eye Health

DHA is concentrated in the retina and is an important structural component of retinal cell membranes.

This biological role has made DHA an important area of nutrition research. However, the presence of DHA in the retina does not mean that taking a high-dose DHA supplement will automatically prevent or cure every eye condition.

People experiencing changes in vision, eye pain, persistent dryness or other eye symptoms should seek appropriate eye care rather than attempting to self-treat with supplements.

Omega-3 and Children's Nutrition

Omega-3 fatty acids are important components of nutrition during childhood. Fish and seafood provide DHA and EPA as well as protein and several vitamins and minerals.

Children should receive age-appropriate amounts of fish selected according to current food-safety guidance. Because mercury exposure is an important consideration for young children, parents should choose fish from lower-mercury categories.

Parents should not give children adult-strength supplements without checking the product's directions and discussing the decision with a healthcare professional when appropriate.

Choosing Fish Safely

Fish can be an excellent source of omega-3, but the species matters because mercury concentrations vary.

Larger, longer-lived predatory fish tend to accumulate more methylmercury. This is why food-safety agencies provide specific recommendations for fish selection, especially for pregnant people, breastfeeding people and children.

Cooking fish does not remove mercury. The safer approach is to choose fish from lower-mercury categories and vary the types of fish eaten.

What About Mercury in Fish?

Mercury is an environmental contaminant that can accumulate in fish. The form of greatest concern in seafood is methylmercury.

Nearly all fish contain at least trace amounts of methylmercury, but levels vary considerably between species. Most fish do not contain mercury at levels expected to cause health effects in adults when eaten according to dietary guidance.

The concern is greater for the developing nervous system, which is why pregnant people and young children receive more specific fish-selection advice.

The goal is not to eliminate fish from the diet. Instead, the goal is to choose nutritious fish that are lower in mercury.

Should You Take Omega-3 Every Day?

Whether a person should take an omega-3 supplement every day depends on the person's diet, health situation, dietary preferences and reason for using the product.

Someone who regularly eats fish may have a different need from someone who never eats seafood. A person with a medical condition may also have different requirements from a healthy adult.

There is no universal rule that every adult needs a high-dose fish-oil supplement every day.

If your diet already provides adequate sources of omega-3, adding a supplement may not provide a meaningful additional benefit. Conversely, people who avoid fish or have specific nutritional needs may discuss alternatives with a healthcare professional.

Omega-3 and a Balanced Diet

Omega-3 is only one part of healthy nutrition. Cardiovascular and overall health are influenced by the entire dietary pattern rather than one nutrient alone.

A balanced eating pattern can include vegetables, fruits, whole grains, beans, nuts, seeds, healthy sources of protein and appropriate amounts of unsaturated fats.

Fish can fit naturally into this type of eating pattern. For people who do not eat fish, foods such as flaxseed, chia seeds and walnuts can contribute ALA.

Common Omega-3 Myths

Myth: All Omega-3 Supplements Are the Same

They are not. Products can contain different combinations and amounts of EPA, DHA and other fats. Some products are fish oil, some are algae oil and others are different formulations.

Myth: More Omega-3 Is Always Better

More is not automatically better. High-dose supplementation can have different effects from obtaining moderate amounts of omega-3 from food.

Myth: Plant Omega-3 Is Exactly the Same as Fish Omega-3

Plant foods such as flaxseed primarily provide ALA, whereas fish and seafood provide EPA and DHA. The body can convert ALA into EPA and DHA, but the conversion is limited.

Myth: Fish Oil Can Replace Heart Medication

It cannot. A supplement should never be used as a substitute for prescribed treatment without medical advice.

Who Should Talk to a Healthcare Professional Before Taking Omega-3?

Although omega-3 is an important part of nutrition, some people should obtain professional advice before starting a concentrated supplement.

  • People taking medicines that affect blood clotting.
  • People with a bleeding disorder.
  • People preparing for surgery.
  • People who are pregnant or breastfeeding.
  • Children who may be given concentrated supplements.
  • People with significant medical conditions.
  • People taking several prescription medicines.

A healthcare professional can review the exact product, dose, medications and individual health circumstances before you begin supplementation.

What Should You Check on an Omega-3 Label?

Shopping for omega-3 can be confusing because manufacturers may highlight the total amount of fish oil while providing less information about the specific omega-3 fatty acids.

When comparing products, look carefully for the amounts of EPA and DHA per serving. Also check the number of capsules that make up the serving size.

  • Total fish oil or algae oil per serving.
  • EPA amount.
  • DHA amount.
  • Serving size.
  • Other active ingredients.
  • Directions for use.
  • Storage instructions.
  • Expiration or best-before information.

The Bottom Line: What Is Omega-3?

Omega-3 refers to a family of polyunsaturated fatty acids that are important components of human nutrition. The three main omega-3 fatty acids discussed in nutrition are ALA, EPA and DHA.

ALA is an essential fatty acid found primarily in plant foods such as flaxseed, chia seeds, walnuts and certain vegetable oils. EPA and DHA are found mainly in fish and seafood, while algae can provide DHA and, depending on the product, EPA.

Omega-3 fatty acids have important biological roles. DHA is especially concentrated in the brain and retina, while EPA and DHA participate in numerous normal cellular processes.

Eating fish and seafood as part of a balanced dietary pattern can provide omega-3 fatty acids together with protein, vitamins and minerals. The American Heart Association recommends eating fish at least twice a week, particularly fatty fish.

Supplements can be useful in particular situations, but they should not automatically be considered necessary for everyone. People taking medication, managing a medical condition, preparing for surgery, or considering supplementation during pregnancy should obtain appropriate professional advice.

The most sensible approach is to look at omega-3 as one part of an overall healthy eating pattern rather than as a miracle nutrient or a substitute for medical treatment.

Key Points

  • Omega-3 is a family of polyunsaturated fatty acids.
  • The three major omega-3 fatty acids are ALA, EPA and DHA.
  • ALA is mainly found in plant foods such as flaxseed, chia seeds and walnuts.
  • EPA and DHA are found mainly in fish and seafood.
  • The body converts only limited amounts of ALA into EPA and DHA.
  • DHA is an important structural component of the brain and retina.
  • Fatty fish are practical dietary sources of EPA and DHA.
  • Fish should be selected with mercury exposure in mind, especially during pregnancy and childhood.
  • Omega-3 supplements are not automatically necessary for everyone.
  • People taking medication or managing medical conditions should discuss concentrated omega-3 supplements with a healthcare professional.
